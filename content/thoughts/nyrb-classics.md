*---NYRB?*

[New York Review Books](https://www.nyrb.com) is the publishing
house of the literary journal and magazine, [*The New York Review
of Books*](https://www.nybooks.com), of which I proudly know
nothing about. However, their publishing branch is excellent, in
my uneducated opinion. Their main imprint is NYRB Classics, which
publishes "an eclectic mix of fiction and nonfiction from
different eras and times and of various sorts."[^1] Many of the
books are either out of print or rarely translated into English,
ripe for a reprint.

If you've been around a particular side of YouTube, you might have
heard praise for John Williams' *Stoner* or seen shelves stacked
with rows of solid, colorful books. That corner is where, more
than three years ago, I heard about the NYRB Book Club, a yearly
subscription that offers one new book from the Classics catalog
each month. It is not a crazy steal, the books just slightly
discounted, but the variety appealed to me. I am not of a worldly,
literary kind. I read "classics" and enjoy them, but that is the
extent of it. What better way to force myself to take a break from
fantastical worlds and read new stuff than a random literary
volume dropped off in the mailbox once a month?

Here, two renewals and thirty-six books later, are my thoughts on
the whole thing.

### ***Friday***, Michel Tournier
- Originally Published 1967
- Rating: 4/5

A retelling of the classic story of Robinson Crusoe. I've never
read Defoe's original work, so this is my first introduction to
the famous story. I thought the book was excellent, very exciting,
and thought-provoking. It's interesting to consider how being
isolated in nature away from any civilization could change
someone, for the better or worse. In this variation, Crusoe stays
on the island after a pagan spiritual transformation. At first
compelled to civilize the island with technology, agriculture,
social structure (even for one person), and religion, Crusoe
abandons the effort in favor of some kind of natural paganism when
he notices Friday's carefree nature.

I've read that this was written for children? Maybe genius
children, or I'm stupid, but I found that the philosophical
digressions in Crusoe's journal were a lot to digest. Interesting
for sure, though, like how we have a prejudice against breadth in
favor of depth, evident in the interpretation of "superficial" as
lacking depth rather than possessing great breadth. Or that we
should love deeply rather than widely.

Another favorite moment:

> Since I have been here I have become something of a specialist
in silence, or in silences, I should say...There are airy, scented
silences like a June night in England, others with the glaucous
thickness of the mire, and yet others that are hard and sonorous
as ebony.

I feel a little guilty for reading this before Dafoe's original.
But oh well, at least when I do get around to it, I'll have a
little extra enthusiasm.

### ***The Universal Baseball Association Inc.***, Robert Coover
- Originally Published 1968
- Rating: 4/5

I have never played or followed baseball. I couldn't describe how
the game is played. First opening this book and reading the title,
I shelved it in my brain beside the rest of the NYRBs that just
weren't *me*. Fortunately for me, this book isn't really about
baseball. Not exactly.

Enter Henry, dead-end accountant. He hates his boss, he's utterly
alone, and in his fifties, he's on the brink of losing his job
because he can't stop playing his fantasy baseball game. And if
you're like me, and you think bets and trades and ridiculous
punishments, you'd be far from the truth. Written in 1968, the
idea of fantasy sports didn't exist. What Henry has created is an
entire alternate world: The Universal Baseball Association. For 55
in-game seasons, Henry has simulated not just the game, but the
league and the politics of baseball, with dice, score cards,
fictional records, characters, and more. Coover's writing brings
the players to life, and the way the real and unreal blend
together shows just how obsessed Henry is.

One of my favorite passages is when Henry invites his only friend,
Lou, to play a few rounds of the game. Greasy pizza fingers on the
scorecards, split beer, and a heretical disrespect for the
association make for an uncomfortable night for Henry. It
perfectly captures the feeling of trying to share a niche personal
obsession with a friend, when everything once magical seems lame
in the moment.

There is a complicated discussion of the art of creation in this
drama, something timeless. The relationship between the art and
the artist is toxic. Henry blames himself for the stagnation of
the league, hoping that one star pitcher might garner new seasons
of vitality. However, when an incredible series of rolls leads to
his death by a stray pitch, Henry loses it all. By the end of the
book, He has started to manipulate the game out of desperation.
Reading this today, you can't help but think about all the
different simulations we can escape to. So whether an artist's
parable, commentary on escapism or religion, or a haunting
prediction of modern sports, this book has it.

> Henry had invented for them a variation on Monopoly, using
twelve, sixteen, or twenty-four boards at once and an unlimited
number of players, which opened up the possibility of wars run by
industrial giants with investments on several boards at once, the
buying off of whole governments ... strikes and rebellions by the
slumdwellers between "Go" and "Jail," revolutionary subversion and
sabotage with sympathetic ties across the boards ... but it never
caught on.
